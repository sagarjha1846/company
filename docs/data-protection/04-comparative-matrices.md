# Part 4 — Comparative matrices

Ten themes, each read across the jurisdictions studied in [Part 3](03-international-landscape.md).
India's column is first in every table so the divergence is visible at a glance.

**Reading.** India is one of only two entries excluding non-digital processing outright, and

publicly available data from the statute's application rather than treating it as a
processing-basis question. Together these are the widest scope gaps in any comprehensive law
studied here.

## 2. Lawful bases for processing

| Jurisdiction | Consent | Contract | Legal obligation | Vital interests | Public task | **Legitimate interests** | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **India** | ✅ | **❌** | ✅ S.7(d) | ⚠️ S.7(f) medical emergency only | ✅ S.7(b),(c) State only | **❌** | **2 categories, 9 enumerated uses** |
| EU | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | 6 |
| UK | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ + statutory "recognised" list | 6+ |
| Brazil | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | 10 |
| China | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ⚠️ | **❌** | 7 |
| Japan | ⚠️ opt-out available | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ⚠️ | flexible |
| South Korea | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ⚠️ narrow | 7 |
| Singapore | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ + business improvement | 8+ |
| US states | ⚠️ opt-out model | n/a | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ implicit | purpose-based |
| Vietnam | ✅ | ⚠️ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ⚠️ | narrow |

**Reading.** India and China are the two majors with **no legitimate-interests basis**. But
China compensates with a contract/HR basis; India has neither. The consequence is structural:
in India, almost every non-State commercial purpose beyond bare order fulfilment must be
consented to. That is why the Consent Manager institution exists, and it is the single most
consequential design decision in the Act.

## 3. Sensitive / special-category data

| Jurisdiction | Special category exists | Includes health | Biometrics | Caste/race/ethnicity | Religion | Sexual orientation | Financial | Children's data as sensitive |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **India (DPDP)** | **❌ none** | ❌ | ❌ | ❌ | ❌ | ❌ | ❌ | ❌ (separate S.9 regime) |
| India (SPDI Rules 2011, superseded) | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ❌ | ❌ | ✅ | ✅ | ❌ |
| EU | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ❌ | ❌ |
| Brazil | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ❌ | ⚠️ |
| China | ✅ harm-based | ✅ | ✅ | ⚠️ "specific identity" | ✅ | ⚠️ | ✅ | ✅ under-14s |
| South Korea | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ⚠️ unique IDs | ⚠️ |
| Thailand | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ❌ | ❌ |
| Nigeria | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ⚠️ | ⚠️ |
| Indonesia | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ❌ | ❌ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ |
| US states | ✅ opt-in | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ⚠️ some | ✅ under-13/16 |
| Vietnam | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ⚠️ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ |

**Reading.** **India is the outlier.** Every other regime studied treats some data as
categorically riskier. India's uniform-protection design is defensible in theory — it avoids
the definitional arbitrage of category lists — but in the Indian context it means **caste,
religious identity, health status and biometric identifiers carry no additional statutory
protection**, in a country where those attributes carry unusually acute discrimination risk and
where Aadhaar has made biometric processing routine. It is also a *regression* from the 2011
SPDI Rules the Act displaced.

## 4. Children's data

| Jurisdiction | Age threshold | Verifiable parental consent | Ban on targeted ads to children | Ban on tracking/behavioural monitoring | Age-assurance mandate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **India** | **< 18** | ✅ (identity details or DigiLocker-style virtual token) | ✅ **absolute** | ✅ absolute, with Fourth Schedule carve-outs | ⚠️ implicit |
| EU | 13–16 (member-state choice) | ✅ for information-society services | ⚠️ via DSA Art. 28 | ⚠️ | ⚠️ |
| UK | 13 | ✅ | ⚠️ AADC + Online Safety Act | ⚠️ | ✅ OSA highly effective age assurance |
| US (COPPA) | < 13 | ✅ | ✅ without consent | ✅ without consent | ⚠️ |
| China | < 14 (sensitive PI) | ✅ | ⚠️ | ⚠️ | ✅ minor mode |
| Japan (2026) | < 16 | ✅ | ⚠️ | ⚠️ | ⚠️ |
| South Korea | < 14 | ✅ | ⚠️ | ⚠️ | ⚠️ |
| Australia | ⚠️ code in development | ⚠️ | ⚠️ | ⚠️ | ✅ under-16 social media ban |
| Brazil | < 18 (best interests) | ✅ | ⚠️ | ⚠️ | ⚠️ |

**Reading.** India sets the **highest age threshold in the world** and pairs it with the
**most categorical advertising and tracking ban**. The practical friction is real: an Indian
platform must treat a 17-year-old the way a US platform treats a 12-year-old, and to know
who is 17 it must engage in identity verification that itself creates privacy risk. The
Fourth Schedule exemptions relieve schools, hospitals and childcare providers, but not
consumer internet services. Expect S.9(5) — the power to notify a lower age for a class of
Fiduciary — to be the pressure valve.

## 5. Data principal / data subject rights

| Right | **India** | EU | UK | Brazil | China | S. Korea | Singapore | US states |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Information / notice | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ |
| Access | ⚠️ **summary only, not a copy** | ✅ copy | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ |
| Correction | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ |
| Erasure | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ⚠️ | ✅ |
| **Portability** | **❌** | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ⚠️ legislated, not in force | ✅ |
| **Object / restrict** | **❌** | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ⚠️ | ⚠️ | ⚠️ | ✅ opt-out |
| **Automated decisions / explanation** | **❌** | ✅ Art. 22 | ⚠️ relaxed by DUAA | ✅ review right | ✅ Art. 24 | ✅ | ❌ | ⚠️ CA/CO ADMT rules |
| Grievance redressal against the fiduciary | ✅ **mandatory first step** | ⚠️ optional | ✅ DUAA duty | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ⚠️ appeal right |
| **Nomination on death/incapacity** | **✅ unique** | ❌ | ❌ | ❌ | ⚠️ | ❌ | ❌ | ❌ |
| Compensation for harm | **❌** | ✅ Art. 82 | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ statutory damages | ✅ private action | ⚠️ CA breach only |
| Enforceable duties **on** the individual | **✅ ₹10,000 penalty — unique** | ❌ | ❌ | ❌ | ❌ | ❌ | ❌ | ❌ |

**Reading.** India's rights set is the thinnest of any comprehensive regime studied, and it is
the only one that runs the accountability arrow in both directions. Two absences matter most
in practice: **portability** (which is what makes competition policy and data-driven switching
work — and which India has actually built infrastructure for, in Account Aggregator and ONDC,
without granting the right) and **automated-decision rights** (in an economy where algorithmic
credit scoring, insurance pricing and welfare eligibility determination are already at scale).

## 6. Breach notification

| Jurisdiction | To regulator | To individuals | Materiality threshold | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **India** | **Without delay + full particulars within 72h** | **Without delay** | **None — every breach** | Cumulative with CERT-In's 6-hour direction |
| EU | 72h where risk likely | Where **high** risk | ✅ risk-based | |
| UK | 72h | High risk | ✅ | |
| Brazil | "reasonable time" (ANPD guidance: 3 working days) | Where relevant risk | ✅ | |
| China | Immediately | Yes, unless mitigated | ⚠️ | |
| Japan | Prompt + 30/60-day report | Yes, for specified leak types | ✅ | |
| South Korea | 72h | 72h | ⚠️ ≥1,000 individuals for some duties | |
| Singapore | 3 calendar days | Yes | ✅ ≥500 people or significant harm | |
| Australia | ASAP, ≤30 days assessment | Yes | ✅ likely serious harm | |
| Indonesia | 3×24h | 3×24h | ❌ | |
| Nigeria | 72h | Where high risk | ⚠️ | |
| Saudi Arabia | 72h | Where harm | ⚠️ | |

**Reading.** India's regime is the strictest in the table on two axes at once: **no
materiality filter** and **immediate individual notification**. For a large consumer platform
this converts routine, low-impact incidents — a misdirected email, a single-record access
anomaly — into mandatory dual notifications carrying a ₹200 crore ceiling for failure. Expect
this to be the first provision the industry seeks to soften, and expect over-notification to be
the observed behaviour in the meantime.

## 7. Cross-border transfers

| Jurisdiction | Default | Mechanisms | Localisation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **India** | **Permitted** | Government **negative list**; conditions on foreign-government access | ⚠️ **SDFs only**, for notified categories; plus sectoral (RBI payments, insurance, telecom) |
| EU | Prohibited | Adequacy, SCCs, BCRs, certification, derogations + transfer impact assessment | ❌ |
| UK | Prohibited | Adequacy ("data bridges"), IDTA/Addendum, BCRs; DUAA introduces a new outcomes-based test | ❌ |
| Brazil | Prohibited | Adequacy, ANPD SCCs, BCRs, consent | ❌ |
| China | Prohibited | CAC security assessment / CAC standard contract + filing / **certification (Measures effective 1 Jan 2026)** | ✅ CIIOs and threshold-exceeding processors |
| Russia | Prohibited | Consent + filing | ✅ hard localisation |
| Vietnam | Permitted with filing | Transfer impact dossier to Ministry of Public Security | ⚠️ |
| Indonesia | Prohibited | Adequacy, safeguards, consent | ❌ |
| Nigeria | Prohibited | Adequacy, safeguards, derogations | ❌ |
| Saudi Arabia | Prohibited | Adequacy or risk assessment | ⚠️ sectoral |
| US states | Permitted | ❌ none | ❌ |

**Reading.** India sits with the US at the permissive end — a deliberate signal to global
services and to India's own IT/BPM export sector (reinforced by the S.17(1)(d) outsourcing
exemption). The latent risk is that the negative list and the SDF localisation categories are
both **unbounded executive powers that can be exercised overnight**, so the permissiveness is
policy, not entitlement. A prudent architecture keeps transfer flows *documentable and
re-routable* even though nothing currently requires a transfer mechanism.

## 8. Regulator design and independence

| Jurisdiction | Regulator | Appointment | Tenure | Independence safeguards | Fining power |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **India** | Data Protection Board | **Central Government**, via Cabinet-Secretary-led search committee | **2 years, renewable** | Weak — renewable short terms, Government-set service conditions, Government power to call for information (S.36) | ✅ but capped in absolute terms |
| EU | National DPAs + EDPB | Parliamentary/independent processes | 4–6 years, typically non-renewable or once | **Treaty-level independence (Art. 8 Charter, Art. 52 GDPR)** | ✅ |
| UK | Information Commission (from ICO) | Crown appointment, parliamentary scrutiny | Fixed | Statutory independence + board governance | ✅ |
| Brazil | ANPD | Presidential appointment with **Senate confirmation** | 4 years, staggered | Autonomous agency since 2022 | ✅ |
| China | CAC | Party-state | n/a | ❌ by design | ✅ |
| South Korea | PIPC | Presidential/National Assembly mix | 3 years | Strong in practice | ✅ |
| Singapore | PDPC | Government (IMDA) | n/a | Moderate | ✅ |
| Australia | OAIC | Governor-General | 5 years | Moderate | ✅ via courts |
| Nigeria | NDPC | Presidential | Fixed | Moderate | ✅ |
| Canada | OPC | Parliamentary appointment | 7 years | Strong | **❌ no fining power** |

**Reading.** **Two-year renewable terms are the weakest tenure protection in the table.** A
member who wishes to be reappointed is structurally disincentivised from ruling against the
Government that reappoints them — precisely when the Board's most important cases will involve
State instrumentalities processing under S.7(b)–(e). Combined with S.17(2)(a) exemptions and
Rule 23 information demands, this is the principal institutional-design criticism of the Indian
regime, and the one most likely to be tested against *Puttaswamy*'s procedural-safeguards limb.

## 9. Penalties and redress

| Jurisdiction | Maximum penalty | Turnover-linked | Compensation to individuals | Private right of action | Class/representative actions | Criminal liability |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **India** | ₹250 crore per instance | **❌ absolute cap** | **❌** | **❌** | ❌ | ❌ |
| EU | €20m / 4% worldwide | ✅ | ✅ Art. 82 | ✅ | ✅ Art. 80 | ⚠️ member-state |
| UK | £17.5m / 4% | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ⚠️ | ⚠️ |
| Brazil | 2% Brazil revenue, cap BRL 50m | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ collective actions | ❌ |
| China | ¥50m / 5% | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ procuratorate public-interest suits | ✅ |
| South Korea | **10% of total revenue (severe breach cases, from 2026)** | ✅ | ✅ statutory damages | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ |
| Singapore | 10% SG turnover / S$1m | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ❌ | ⚠️ |
| Australia | A$50m / 30% adjusted turnover | ✅ | ⚠️ | **✅ statutory tort since Jun 2025** | ✅ | ⚠️ doxxing |
| US (CA) | Per-violation civil penalties | ❌ | ⚠️ | ⚠️ breach only; **✅ under My Health My Data / BIPA** | ✅ | ❌ |
| Thailand | THB 5m | ❌ | ✅ + punitive damages ×2 | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ |
| South Africa | ZAR 10m | ❌ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ up to 10 years |

**Reading.** India's headline number is large and its **structure is weak**. Absolute caps
under-deter the largest firms and over-deter mid-market Indian ones. More importantly, India is
the only jurisdiction in this table where an individual harmed by a data breach has **no
statutory monetary remedy of any kind** — the penalty goes to the Consolidated Fund, S.43A of
the IT Act was repealed, and S.39 bars civil courts from matters the Board can decide.
Deterrence rests entirely on a regulator that does not yet exist.

## 10. Accountability obligations on organisations

| Obligation | **India** | EU | UK | Brazil | China | Singapore | Nigeria |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Records of processing | ⚠️ **SDF only** | ✅ (>250 staff or risk) | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ⚠️ | ✅ |
| DPO | ⚠️ **SDF only** (must be India-resident, board-reporting) | ✅ risk-based | ✅ "senior responsible individual" | ✅ all controllers | ✅ threshold | ✅ **all organisations** | ✅ threshold |
| DPIA | ⚠️ **SDF only, annual** | ✅ high-risk | ✅ | ⚠️ ANPD may require | ✅ specified processing | ⚠️ recommended | ✅ |
| Independent audit | ✅ **SDF only, annual** | ❌ | ❌ | ❌ | ✅ periodic compliance audit | ❌ | ✅ annual returns |
| Privacy by design/default | ❌ | ✅ Art. 25 | ✅ | ⚠️ | ⚠️ | ⚠️ | ⚠️ |
| Prescribed security controls | **✅ Rule 6 — encryption, access control, ≥1-year logs, backups** | ❌ risk-based only | ❌ | ❌ | ✅ MLPS graded scheme | ⚠️ guidance | ✅ |
| Processor contract mandated | ✅ S.8(2) | ✅ Art. 28 | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ | ✅ |
| Algorithmic risk due diligence | ✅ **SDF only, Rule 13** | ⚠️ via AI Act | ⚠️ | ⚠️ | ✅ Art. 24 | ❌ | ❌ |

**Reading.** India inverts the usual pattern. Most regimes impose light-but-universal
accountability duties (records, DPO, DPIA for everyone above a threshold). India imposes
**almost nothing on the ordinary Fiduciary** and a **heavy, audited package on a small
Government-designated set of SDFs** — plus one universal, prescriptive, engineering-level
security rule. The result is a compliance landscape with a very sharp cliff at the SDF
boundary, and with Rule 6 as the only universal, testable technical standard. For any company
prioritising work before May 2027, that makes Rule 6 the correct first investment.
