# Part 16 — Competition law got there first

The most important privacy enforcement in India to date was not done by a data protection
regulator. It was done by the **Competition Commission of India**, against Meta, over WhatsApp's
privacy policy — and it delivered, through competition law, almost exactly the consent standard
that S.6 of the DPDP Act will require.

This is India's **Meta v. Bundeskartellamt**, and it arrived while the Data Protection Board
still had no members.

---

## 1. What happened

**The conduct.** In January 2021 WhatsApp rolled out an updated privacy policy that mandated
sharing user data with Meta group companies, on a take-it-or-leave-it basis: accept, or lose the
service.

**The CCI order (November 2024).** The Commission found abuse of dominance and:

- imposed a penalty of **₹213.14 crore**;
- **barred WhatsApp from sharing user data with other Meta companies for advertising purposes
  for five years**; and
- held that **sharing user data for purposes other than providing the WhatsApp service could not
  be made a precondition** for accessing WhatsApp in India.

**The NCLAT appeal (4 November 2025).** The Tribunal **set aside the five-year advertising
data-sharing ban** but **upheld the ₹213 crore penalty**. (At the interim stage it had stayed
the ban and directed a deposit of half the fine.)

**The Supreme Court (2026).** Meta and WhatsApp moved the Supreme Court in January 2026 against
the NCLAT order upholding the penalty. In February the Court warned the platforms in strong
terms about playing with citizens' privacy in the name of data sharing. On **23 February 2026**
the companies undertook to **implement the required safeguards — a user-consent-based framework
for data sharing — by 16 March 2026**, and withdrew the interim application seeking to resist
enforcement of that direction. **The main appeal remains pending.**

---

## 2. Why it matters doctrinally

### 2.1 A competition regulator enforced a data protection norm

The CCI's central holding — that data sharing beyond service provision **cannot be a
precondition of access** — is functionally identical to what S.6(1) of the DPDP Act requires:
consent must be **free** and **unconditional**, and is **void to the extent** it extends beyond
the data necessary for the specified purpose.

India therefore has, in substance, its first enforcement of the DPDP consent standard — reached
under the Competition Act, 2002, two and a half years before the standard itself becomes
enforceable.

### 2.2 It tracks the European reasoning

**Case C-252/21 *Meta Platforms v. Bundeskartellamt*** (4 July 2023) held that a competition
authority **may** find a GDPR infringement incidentally when assessing abuse of dominance, and
that a controller's **dominant position is relevant to whether consent is freely given**. The
CCI reached the same destination on Indian facts.

The convergence is not coincidental: both regimes are answering the same question — whether
consent extracted by a firm the user cannot practically leave is consent at all.

### 2.3 It shows where enforcement will actually come from

With S.27–S.34 uncommenced and the Board unstaffed, the institutions with functioning
enforcement machinery over data practices in India today are the **CCI**, **sectoral regulators**
(RBI, SEBI, IRDAI, TRAI), **CERT-In**, and the **courts**. That is where privacy outcomes will be
produced through 2026–27, and it is a materially different risk map from the one a company
planning only for the Board would draw.

---

## 3. The overlap problem this creates

Once the Board is functioning, the same conduct can attract **two regulators applying two
statutes to one set of facts**:

| | CCI, Competition Act, 2002 | Board, DPDP Act, 2023 |
|---|---|---|
| Question asked | Is consent-extraction an abuse of dominance? | Is the consent free, specific and unconditional? |
| Trigger | Dominance in a relevant market | Any Data Fiduciary, dominant or not |
| Remedy | Penalty on **turnover**, behavioural remedies | Penalty to an **absolute cap**, remedial directions |
| Who benefits | Market structure | The individual — though not financially |
| Status today | **Operational and enforcing** | Not yet operational |

**Neither statute resolves the overlap.** S.38(1) of the DPDP Act makes it *additional to* other
laws, and S.38(2) makes a higher standard elsewhere prevail — which points toward cumulative
application rather than exclusivity. The realistic expectation is **parallel proceedings** on
the same facts, with the competition penalty likely to be the larger one because it scales with
turnover while the DPDP Schedule does not.

There is also a **forum question** nobody has answered: S.39 of the DPDP Act bars civil courts
from matters the Board may determine, but says nothing about the CCI, whose jurisdiction rests
on a separate statute and whose appeals go to the NCLAT rather than the TDSAT. Two appellate
tracks over overlapping conduct is an obvious source of inconsistency.

A **digital competition framework** — following the Committee on Digital Competition Law's
recommendations for ex-ante obligations on large digital enterprises, including limits on
cross-service data combination — has been proposed but **not enacted**. If it arrives, it will
add a third layer addressing much the same conduct.

---

## 4. What this means practically

1. **Do not assume the Board's absence means no enforcement risk.** The largest privacy penalty
   in Indian history to date was levied by a competition regulator, and the Supreme Court is
   actively supervising compliance.
2. **Take-it-or-leave-it consent is the exposed pattern.** It is simultaneously an abuse-of-
   dominance theory, a S.6(1) invalidity, and — per *Meta v. Bundeskartellamt* — the position
   most comparable regimes have converged on. If your product conditions access on consent to
   secondary data use, that is the first thing to redesign.
3. **Cross-service data combination is the specific conduct under scrutiny** — sharing between
   group companies for advertising or profiling, where the user has no meaningful alternative.
4. **If you are dominant in any Indian market, your consent design is a competition-law
   artefact**, not just a privacy one, and should be reviewed by both benches of advice.
5. **Watch the pending Supreme Court appeal.** A ruling on the interaction between competition
   law and informational privacy would shape how the Board reads S.6 from its first day of
   operation — much as *Bundeskartellamt* now shapes European consent analysis.
