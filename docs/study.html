01The short version

India has enacted a short, delegation-heavy, consent-maximalist statute with strong front-end obligations and weak back-end accountability. The engineering duties are more prescriptive than the GDPR’s; the legal exposure is lower, and far less predictable.

Forty-four sections across nine chapters, plus one Schedule — roughly a fifth the length of the GDPR. Twenty-six subjects are delegated to rules, and the Rules in turn leave core parameters to future notification. A company cannot fully determine its obligations from the statute book.

Eight findings

  1. Two lawful bases, not six. Consent S.6 or one of nine enumerated “certain legitimate uses” S.7. No legitimate-interests basis. No contractual-necessity basis. Ordinary commercial processing runs on consent.
  2. Sensitive personal data was abolished. India is the only major jurisdiction with no special category — a regression from the 2011 SPDI Rules it displaced. Caste, religion, health and biometrics carry no additional statutory protection.
  3. Children’s data is the strictest limb. Verifiable parental consent under 18 — the highest threshold in the world — with an absolute ban on tracking and targeted advertising, and a ₹200 crore ceiling.
  4. Cross-border transfer is the most liberal limb. A negative list: data may go anywhere until the Government says otherwise. No adequacy, no SCCs, no transfer impact assessments.
  5. Breach notification is stricter than the GDPR. No materiality threshold, and notification to every affected individual “without delay,” on top of CERT-In’s separate six-hour clock.
  6. The regulator cannot yet act. The sections giving the Board its functions, procedure and penalty powers — S.27–34 — were not commenced on 13 November 2025, and no Chairperson or Member has been appointed to exercise them if they had been. Terms are two years, renewable, at the Government’s hand.
  7. Competition law got there first. The largest privacy enforcement in India to date was the CCI’s ₹213.14 crore order against Meta over WhatsApp’s 2021 policy — holding that data sharing beyond service provision cannot be a precondition of access, which is functionally what S.6(1) requires. NCLAT upheld the penalty; the Supreme Court is supervising compliance.
  8. The individual has no monetary remedy — from May 2027. Penalties go to the Consolidated Fund and S.39 bars civil courts. But S.44(2), which deletes the S.43A IT Act compensation claim, has not commenced — so that claim and the SPDI Rules, 2011 still stand. India runs two data protection regimes at once until the substantive phase.
  9. The RTI amendment is before a five-judge Constitution Bench. S.44(3) deleted the public-interest override from S.8(1)(j) RTI Act. The Supreme Court issued notice, refused an interim stay, and referred the challenge to a Constitution Bench.

02The Act

The Act is the legislative answer to Puttaswamy (2017), which held informational privacy to be a fundamental right under Article 21 and supplied the proportionality test — legality, legitimate aim, necessity, proportionality, procedural safeguards — against which the Act’s own carve-outs are now being measured in court.

Everything distinctive about the statute dates from August 2022, when the Personal Data Protection Bill, 2019 was withdrawn in its entirety and redrawn: no sensitive-data categories, no hard localisation, a tiny lawful-basis list, and substance delegated to rules.

The nine legitimate uses

These are exhaustive, and the shape of the list is the point: six of the nine are State grounds, one is employment, and only S.7(a) — data “voluntarily provided” for a specified purpose — is a flexible commercial ground. The State gets a self-executing basis for essentially all citizen-facing service delivery; a private company gets nothing equivalent for its own.

Structural note

These are absolute caps, not turnover-linked. ₹250 crore is roughly US$29 million — a cost of doing business for a global platform, and existential for an Indian mid-market firm. The schedule is regressive in effect, and S.42 lets the Government raise the amounts only to twice the stated figure.

The RTI amendment

Section 8(1)(j) of the Right to Information Act formerly exempted personal information only where disclosure had no relationship to public activity or would be an unwarranted invasion of privacy — unless the larger public interest justified disclosure. S.44(3) replaces the whole clause with six words: “information which relates to personal information.”

Three elements were deleted: the public-activity nexus test, the unwarranted-invasion test, and the public-interest override. Public Information Officers lose the power to balance privacy against accountability — a balancing exercise the Constitution Bench in CPIO v. Subhash Chandra Agarwal (2020) had held the clause required.

Petitions by the National Campaign for People’s Right to Information, RTI activist Venkatesh Nayak and The Reporters’ Collective are pending. A bench of Chief Justice Surya Kant, Bagchi and Pancholi JJ issued notice, declined to stay the amendment, and referred the matter to a five-judge Constitution Bench. Beyond the RTI point they challenge the complete absence of a journalism exemption under Article 19(1)(a), and the breadth of S.17(2)(a).

Why the referral matters more than the notice

A Constitution Bench is not confined to S.44(3). Once the question is framed as the constitutional balance between privacy and transparency, S.17(2)(a), the missing press exemption and Rule 23’s gag are all within reach — measured against the proportionality test from Puttaswamy (2017), whose third and fourth limbs, necessity and procedural safeguards, are exactly what those provisions omit. But Constitution Bench listings are slow, and a ruling is unlikely before obligations commence on 13 May 2027. Build to the Act as written; do not plan around the judgment.

03The Rules and the clock

Twenty-three rules and seven Schedules, notified 14 November 2025. They carry the detail the Act deliberately omitted: notice format, security controls, breach timings, retention periods, children’s consent mechanics, SDF duties, and the Board’s own machinery. Commencement is staggered across eighteen months.

Phase 1 — in force
13 Nov 2025

Rules 1, 2, 17–22 and Act sections 1(2), 2, 18–26, 35, 38–43, 44(1) and 44(3). The Board is constituted — but not S.27–34, which give it functions, procedure and penalties.

Phase 2
13 Nov 2026

Rule 4. Consent Manager registration opens — Indian incorporation, ₹2 crore net worth, interoperable platform, fit-and-proper test.

Phase 3 — the deadline
13 May 2027

Rules 3, 5–16, 23. Everything substantive: notice, security, breach, erasure, children, rights, transfers, SDF duties.

Read the phasing correctly

Nothing substantive binds an ordinary business before 13 May 2027 — but consent re-collection, re-noticing every pre-commencement consent under S.5(2), retention engineering and breach tooling all have long lead times, and the Consent Manager market gets only six months of operation before the obligations land.

The deadline may move — watch this

On 23 January 2026 MeitY circulated proposals to compress the compliance window for Significant Data Fiduciaries from 18 months to 12, moving SDF compliance from 13 May 2027 to 13 November 2026; to notify the SDF list or classification criteria (reported targets: large platforms, major BFSI, large intermediaries); and to enforce the SDF cross-border restriction immediately on designation. Comments closed 4 February 2026.

Still a proposal as at 19 August 2026 — not notified. The operative dates remain 13 November 2026 for Consent Managers and 13 May 2027 for full compliance. But it signals direction: SDFs first, faster, with localisation live from day one. Any plausible SDF should plan to November 2026, and should assume the localisation duty arrives with the designation rather than after a grace period.

The four rules that will define compliance

Rule 3 — notice

The notice must be presented independently of any other information, in clear and plain language, with an itemised description of the data and the purposes, and the means to withdraw consent, exercise rights and complain to the Board — in English or any of the 22 Eighth Schedule languages, at the individual’s option. This is the end of the bundled privacy policy in India.

Rule 6 — security safeguards

India did what the GDPR pointedly avoided: it named the controls. Encryption, obfuscation, masking or tokenisation; access control; logs and monitoring of access retained for at least one year; backups sufficient for continued processing after compromise; equivalent safeguards imposed contractually on processors. It is the most auditable rule in the framework, and it carries the ₹250 crore tier.

Rule 7 — breach notification

To every affected individual, without delay, in plain language. To the Board, without delay in outline and with full particulars within 72 hours. No materiality threshold — every breach, however small. Cumulative with CERT-In’s six-hour incident report.

Behind all three sits the ₹250 crore tier for the underlying security failure — independent exposures, so notifying perfectly does not cure a control failure and good controls do not excuse a late notice. The trap is reportability: CERT-In’s trigger is a listed cyber incident; the DPDP trigger is any personal data breach with no threshold. Most personal data breaches are not CERT-In incidents, and some CERT-In incidents involve no personal data. You need two decision trees, not one — and because the second fires often, the process has to be cheap to run or it will not be run.

Rule 13 — Significant Data Fiduciaries

Annual DPIA and independent audit reported to the Board; due diligence that algorithmic software poses no risk to Data Principals’ rights — India’s only AI provision, and undefined; and a duty not to transfer Government-notified data categories or their traffic data outside India. SDF status arrives by notification, not self-assessment: a company cannot know it is one until told.

The enforcement gap

India has a fully notified framework, a fixed deadline and penalty ceilings among the highest in Asia — and no enforcement. The gap is doubled: the sections that would let the Board hear a complaint or impose a penalty were never commenced, and nobody has been appointed to exercise them. Every quarter the Board remains unconstituted compresses the runway before May 2027, when the obligations of several million Data Fiduciaries become enforceable at once.

04Nineteen regimes

Roughly 160 countries now have a comprehensive data protection law. These are the ones that matter for an Indian company: the template India drew from, the template it rejected, the markets it sells into, and the peer economies that legislated in the same window and chose differently.

European Union

GDPR · 2018

Six lawful bases including legitimate interests; special categories; full rights set with portability and Article 22 automated-decision rights; DPIAs, DPOs, records; transfers prohibited by default.

Digital Omnibus Proposed 19 Nov 2025. The AI limb was adopted by Council on 29 June 2026; the GDPR limb is still in negotiation and nothing has entered into force.

United Kingdom

UK GDPR + DUAA 2025

Divergence by simplification: statutory “recognised legitimate interests,” relaxed automated-decision rules, a “stop the clock” on subject access, and a new controller duty to handle complaints from 19 June 2026. The ICO becomes the Information Commission.

Loosened obligations — but kept compensation and an independent regulator. India loosened both.

China

PIPL · 2021

No legitimate-interests basis either — but a contract and HR basis, separate consent for sensitive data and transfers, a harm-based sensitive-data definition, and Article 24 automated-decision rights.

1 Jan 2026 Cross-border transfer certification measures in force; supplementary standards mandatory from 1 July 2026.

Japan

APPI · 2026 amendment

Passed the Diet 10 July 2026, promulgated 17 July, in force within two years. Creates a statistical-processing consent exemption for AI development, parental consent under 16, a new “Specific Biometric Personal Information” category, and administrative fines for the PPC.

South Korea

PIPA · 2026 amendment

Portability, automated-decision rights, and a genuinely independent commission. February 2026 amendments authorise fines up to 10% of total revenue in severe breach cases, with CEO accountability and a mandated CPO; most provisions effective 11 September 2026.

Brazil

LGPD · 2020

The most faithful GDPR transposition outside Europe — ten lawful bases, sensitive categories, portability, review of automated decisions, and an autonomous ANPD. Proof that a developing economy can adopt the full model. India chose not to.

United States

Sectoral + 21 state laws

No comprehensive federal law. Twenty state laws in effect through 2026, with Indiana, Kentucky and Rhode Island commencing 1 January; Alabama enacted the 21st framework and Vermont’s law was signed 16 June 2026. Plus BIPA, My Health My Data, and FTC Act §5.

The fragmentation India avoided — and the enforcement culture it did not import.

Australia

Privacy Act 1988 · 2024 amendments

A statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy since 10 June 2025, a children’s code, doxxing offences and tiered penalties. Tranche 2 — removing the small-business exemption and adding erasure — remains an unlegislated commitment as of mid-2026.

Vietnam

PDPL 91/2025 · 1 Jan 2026

Legislated in the same window as India’s rules and went the other way: filing-based transfer control, an express ban on buying and selling personal data, and penalties of 5% of revenue for transfer violations or ten times the illicit gain. A five-year small-business exemption is in the statute itself.

Nigeria

NDPA 2023 + GAID 2025

The GAID took effect 19 September 2025 with thresholds for “major importance” entities, annual audit returns and licensed compliance organisations. The NDPC issued compliance notices to 1,368 organisations in 2025 — a younger regime enforcing while India’s Board had no members.

Singapore

PDPA · 2014, amended 2020

The pragmatic middle path India’s industry wanted: legitimate interests and business-improvement exceptions, deemed consent, breach notification above a harm threshold, and a DPO in every organisation. India omitted both the exception and the universal DPO.

Canada, Indonesia, UAE

Three cautionary cases

Bill C-27 died on prorogation in January 2025, leaving PIPEDA and a commissioner with no fining power. Indonesia’s law bit in October 2024 with a delayed authority. The UAE’s federal PDPL waited years for executive regulations while the DIFC and ADGM did the real work.

A law that sits unenforced trains the market to ignore it.

Also studied: Switzerland (revFADP, criminal fines on individuals rather than corporate penalties), Thailand, South Africa, Kenya, and Saudi Arabia — where PDPL breaches of sensitive data carry imprisonment.

06The Act is not the whole stack

S.38 makes the DPDP Act additional to every other law, and provides that where another law confers a higher degree of protection or restriction, that higher standard prevails. It is a floor, not a ceiling — and in the sectors where Indian companies actually operate, it is frequently the least demanding instrument in the stack.

Two further points belong here. The IT Amendment Rules, 2026 cut the intermediary takedown clock from 36 hours to three hours, and removal of non-consensual intimate or morphed imagery — deepfakes included — from 24 hours to two hours. And S.72A of the IT Act, criminalising disclosure of personal information in breach of a lawful contract, is untouched by S.44 and survives permanently: after May 2027 it is the only provision in Indian law attaching personal criminal consequence to data misuse, because the DPDP Act creates no offences at all.

Three lessons. Enforcement concentrates where establishment is, and India’s single centralised Board reproduces that concentration by design. The lag between statute and enforcement is normal and then ends abruptly — the GDPR’s first two years were quiet, and over 60% of all fines have landed since 2023, so an inert Indian Board in 2026 predicts nothing about 2028. And volume enforcement lands on the mid-market for ordinary failures — CCTV, marketing, security — not on headline penalties against large platforms.

Where enforcement is actually coming from

In November 2024 the Competition Commission of India fined Meta ₹213.14 crore over WhatsApp’s 2021 privacy policy, barred data sharing with Meta companies for advertising for five years, and held that such sharing could not be a precondition of using WhatsApp in India. NCLAT set the advertising ban aside in November 2025 but upheld the penalty. Meta and WhatsApp moved the Supreme Court; in February 2026 they undertook to implement a user-consent-based data-sharing framework by 16 March 2026 and withdrew their interim application. The main appeal is pending.

This is India’s Meta v. Bundeskartellamt: a competition regulator enforcing, in substance, the DPDP consent standard two and a half years before it becomes enforceable. With S.27–34 uncommenced and the Board unstaffed, the institutions actually policing data practices today are the CCI, the sectoral regulators, CERT-In and the courts — a materially different risk map from the one a company planning only for the Board would draw. And the competition penalty scales with turnover, where the DPDP Schedule does not.

Employment is the one place India is more permissive than the GDPR. S.7(i) makes employment purposes — and safeguarding the employer from loss or liability — a consent-free legitimate use, where EU employee consent is generally treated as invalid for power-imbalance reasons. It removes only the consent requirement, though: not lawful purpose, security, erasure, notice, employee rights, or Puttaswamy proportionality. Commentators argue blanket surveillance clauses are void, not merely risky, since a contract cannot manufacture a legitimate use the section does not supply. Indian employees also have no automated-decision rights — no explanation, no contest, no human review of algorithmic performance scoring.

The practical lesson

For a regulated entity — bank, NBFC, insurer, broker, telco, hospital — the DPDP Act is rarely the binding constraint. The sectoral regulator already exceeds it, and is enforcing today while the Board is not. For an unregulated consumer internet company, DPDP plus the IT Rules is the whole stack. Either way the design conclusion is identical: build the retention and erasure engine around a field-level map of statutory retention obligations. That map is what reconciles everything above it.

07The DPDP Act is India’s AI law

India is not enacting a separate AI statute. The India AI Governance Guidelines, published by MeitY on 5 November 2025 — nine days before the Rules — govern AI through instruments that already exist: the IT Act, the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, consumer law, and the DPDP Act. Seven sutras, an AI Governance Group, a Technology and Policy Expert Committee, an IndiaAI Safety Institute, voluntary compliance and graded liability. The practical consequence: for anything touching personal data, the DPDP Act is the operative AI law — and it was not drafted with models in mind.

The training-data question

S.3(c)(ii) removes from the Act’s application any personal data made public by the individual, or by someone under a legal obligation to publish it. This is the widest such carve-out in comparative law: elsewhere — Singapore, China, several US states — public availability relieves the consent requirement while the data stays inside the statute. In India it leaves the statute entirely, taking every downstream right with it.

Four limits are argued and none has been tested: it is not a public-internet exemption (the trigger is publication by the principal or under legal obligation, not crawlability); the context of publication may not stretch to bulk ingestion; aggregation with non-exempt data produces a composite that is no longer “the published record”; and it says nothing about outputs that identify a person. Treat it as a narrow, provenance-based exemption and keep a dataset register — a corpus whose provenance you cannot evidence is not exempt merely because it was reachable.

The Indian anomaly

India has simultaneously the most restrictive lawful-basis regime (consent only — no legitimate interests, so service consent does not stretch to training) and the most permissive public-data exclusion. That combination pushes every serious Indian AI effort toward the same strategy: assemble corpora that fall outside the Act, and document provenance to prove it. The protection of Indian personal data in AI turns almost entirely on the meaning of one sub-clause.

2026 is the year the AI-regulation wave broke. The EU deferred its own core obligations, Colorado repealed its law before it took effect, and the US federal posture turned deregulatory — while Korea’s Act commenced, China’s labelling rules bit, and Japan legislated a targeted permission rather than a restriction. India’s choice to regulate AI through data protection now looks less like a gap and more like the median position, but only because everyone else moved toward it.

Rule 13’s algorithmic due diligence duty is the only provision aimed squarely at algorithms, and it is supply-side only: a duty on the deployer with no corresponding right for the individual — no explanation, no contest, no human review — undefined as to method, and applying to nobody until an SDF is notified. Compare GDPR Art. 22, PIPL Art. 24 and Korea’s PIPA, each of which gives the individual something.

08Seven structural divergences

Where India departs not from the GDPR specifically, but from the settled practice of every comparable regime.

  1. Penalties on the individual whose data is protected

    S.15 imposes enforceable duties on the Data Principal, and a ₹10,000 penalty for a “false or frivolous” complaint. No other data protection statute in the world does this. Small in money, large in chilling effect — the person deciding what is frivolous is a Government-appointed Board, and the complainant must already have exhausted the Fiduciary’s own grievance process.

  2. Executive control over the regulator

    Two-year renewable terms, Government-set qualifications and service conditions, and a Cabinet-Secretary-chaired selection committee. A member seeking reappointment is structurally disincentivised from ruling against the Government that reappoints them — precisely when the Board’s hardest cases will involve State instrumentalities.

  3. Wholesale State exemptions with no safeguards

    S.17(2)(a) lets the Government exempt any instrumentality of the State on Article 19(2)-style grounds — with no necessity test, no proportionality test, no time limit, no independent review, no notification. GDPR Article 23 permits restrictions only where they respect the essence of the right and specify purposes, categories, safeguards and storage periods. India’s has none of that architecture.

  4. An information-demand power with a statutory gag

    S.36 and Rule 23 let the Government demand information from any Fiduciary or intermediary and forbid disclosure of the demand. No judicial authorisation, no transparency reporting, no sunset, no notification of affected individuals — even after the fact.

  5. No graded data categories

    The law cannot distinguish a newsletter list from a genetic database, so the entire risk-differentiation job falls on SDF designation — which is a Government notification, not a self-assessed threshold.

  6. Penalty design that under-deters the largest actors

    Absolute ceilings rather than a share of turnover. The GDPR’s 4%, Korea’s 10% and China’s 5% scale with the offender; ₹250 crore does not.

  7. Delegation as a governing method

    Which entities are SDFs, which data must stay in India, which countries are restricted, which classes get startup exemptions, what lower age applies to whom — all left to future notification. Obligations are not self-assessable from the text, which is the deepest divergence from the EU model.

09What India got right

The drafting is not uniformly weak, and a fair account has to say so.

  • Rule 6 is the best-drafted security provision in any statute studied. Naming encryption, access control, a one-year log floor, backups and contractual flow-down converts an unfalsifiable “appropriate measures” standard into something an auditor and an engineer can both act on. Article 32 GDPR has produced a decade of argument about what “appropriate” means; Rule 6 will not.
  • Consent quality is textually stronger than the GDPR’s — “unconditional,” plus automatic voiding of consent beyond necessity, plus withdrawal parity, plus the standalone itemised notice.
  • The Consent Manager is an original institution — a registered intermediary that holds no data but brokers, records and revokes consent across services. It is the only serious legislated answer anyone has to consent fatigue, and it builds on India’s tested Account Aggregator and DEPA architecture.
  • The right to nominate S.14 — designating someone to exercise your rights on death or incapacity. Simple, obviously useful, absent everywhere else.
  • The children’s regime is the most protective in the world on paper, and the Fourth Schedule genuinely tries to carve out the legitimate institutional cases — schools, hospitals, childcare, school transport — rather than blanket-banning or blanket-permitting.
  • The negative-list transfer regime avoids the Schrems trap. The GDPR’s transfer architecture has consumed enormous compliance effort for contestable privacy gain, and India declined to replicate it.
  • The 48-hour pre-erasure notice in the Third Schedule prevents silent destruction of a dormant user’s data. No other regime has it.
Where the field is heading

2025–26 is a period of simplification plus harder enforcement. The EU’s Digital Omnibus, the UK’s DUAA and Japan’s AI-oriented APPI amendment all loosen obligations; Korea’s 10%-of-revenue penalties, Nigeria’s 1,368-entity sweep and China’s tightened Cybersecurity Law all raise the cost of non-compliance. India arrives into that environment with light obligations, heavy nominal penalties and no operational regulator — the opposite ordering.

10What to build

The obligations are knowable today, they are testable, and the deadline is fixed. Build to the obligations, not to the enforcement risk.

What a regime like this costs

The GDPR is the closest natural experiment, and the peer-reviewed record is consistent: the losses come through a cost channel, not lost demand.

Stated fairly: these measure costs, not net welfare — none prices the breaches avoided or the trust gained — they are short-run estimates around the 2018 shock, and the GDPR’s obligation set differs from India’s in both directions. Expect a real cost-channel hit concentrated on mid-market firms, expect vendor concentration, and do not expect the sky to fall.

Three features make the burden more regressive in India: penalties are absolute rather than turnover-linked; there is no legitimate-interests basis, so consent infrastructure is mandatory for everyone; and the 22-language notice requirement is a fixed cost independent of size. The statutory counterweight — S.17(3) class and startup exemptions, S.17(4) deferrals of up to five years — remains unused. Meanwhile EY India’s 2026 readiness survey reports 64% of organisations have no dedicated compliance budget and only 18% have completed a cost estimate.

Every lever still unpulled

Each of these changes obligations by notification alone, with no further legislation: Board appointments; commencement of S.27–34; the SDF list or criteria; SDF localisation categories; the restricted-country list; a lower age under S.9(5); startup exemptions under S.17(3); deferrals under S.17(4); State exemptions under S.17(2)(a); and commencement of S.44(2), which ends the last individual compensation route.

Already run a GDPR programme? Seven things it will not cover

  1. Standalone itemised notices in 22 languages — the GDPR requires more content; India requires a particular form.
  2. Consent where the EU uses legitimate interests or contract — analytics, personalisation, product improvement, most fraud analytics.
  3. Rule 6’s named controls, especially the one-year access-log floor and mandatory encryption or tokenisation. Article 32 compliance does not prove Rule 6 compliance.
  4. No-threshold breach notification to every affected individual, without delay — plus the separate CERT-In six-hour report.
  5. Verifiable parental consent for everyone under 18, with the tracking and targeted-ads ban.
  6. Consent Manager integration from 13 November 2026.
  7. The right to nominate S.14.

And four the EU requires that India does not — portability, automated-decision rights, privacy by design, and restriction and objection. Build them anyway if you serve the EU, UK, Brazil, Korea or California; the first two are also the most likely to arrive in India by amendment.

Building once for many jurisdictions

Build to the union of India and the EU and you cover most of the world. The two India-specific builds nothing else gives you are 22-language standalone itemised notices and no-threshold immediate breach notification to every affected individual. The two things India does not give you are portability and automated-decision transparency — build both anyway if you serve the EU, UK, Brazil, Korea or California.
