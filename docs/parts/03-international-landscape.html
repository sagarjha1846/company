Part 3 — Comparable statutes in other jurisdictions

Roughly 160 countries now have a comprehensive data protection law. This part studies the nineteen that matter most for an Indian company: either because they are the template India drew from, the template India rejected, a market an Indian company will sell into, or a peer economy that legislated in the same window and chose differently.

Each entry follows the same shape: instrument → scope → lawful bases → distinctive features → enforcement → what this means for the India comparison.

A. The template: European Union

GDPR (Regulation (EU) 2016/679), applicable since 25 May 2018

Scope. Establishment-based (Art. 3(1)) and targeting-based (Art. 3(2)): offering goods or services to people in the EU or monitoring their behaviour. Covers personal data by any means of processing, digital or structured manual filing.

Lawful bases (Art. 6). Six: consent; contract; legal obligation; vital interests; public task; legitimate interests. The last is the workhorse of commercial processing and requires a documented three-part balancing test.

Special categories (Art. 9). Racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, trade union membership, genetic data, biometric data for identification, health, sex life, sexual orientation — prohibited unless one of ten conditions applies.

Rights. Information (13–14), access with a copy of the data (15), rectification (16), erasure/right to be forgotten (17), restriction (18), portability (20), objection (21), and Art. 22 rights concerning solely automated decisions producing legal or similarly significant effects, including a right to human intervention and to contest.

Accountability machinery. Records of processing (30), security (32), DPIAs for high-risk processing (35), prior consultation (36), DPOs (37–39), privacy by design and by default (25), breach notification to the supervisory authority within 72 hours where risk is likely (33) and to individuals where risk is high (34).

Transfers (Ch. V). Prohibited by default; permitted via adequacy decisions, standard contractual clauses, binding corporate rules, codes/certifications, or narrow derogations — and, since Schrems II, only after a transfer impact assessment of destination-country surveillance law.

Enforcement. Independent supervisory authorities in each member state, the EDPB for consistency, the one-stop-shop for cross-border cases. Fines to €20 million or 4% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher. Art. 82 gives individuals a right to compensation for material and non-material damage, and Art. 80 permits representative actions.

2025–26 development — the Digital Omnibus. On 19 November 2025 the Commission proposed a package amending the GDPR, ePrivacy, the Data Act, NIS2 and DORA, aiming at simplification: narrowing the definition of personal data toward a relative-identifiability standard, easing special-category and AI-training constraints, consolidating cookie consent, and trimming record-keeping. Status as of mid-2026: the AI Omnibus limb was adopted by the Council on 29 June 2026; the Data Omnibus limb, which carries the GDPR changes, is still in negotiation and is not expected to be finally adopted before late 2026 at the earliest. In the Council's June 2026 compromise text, the single-click cookie-rejection and consent- moratorium provisions were dropped after member states failed to agree. Nothing GDPR-related has entered into force.

India comparison. India took the vocabulary (fiduciary/controller, processor, purpose limitation, breach notification, DPIA-for-high-risk) and rejected the machinery: no legitimate interests, no special categories, no portability, no Art. 22, no compensation right, no independent-by-design regulator, no adequacy system. India's regime is GDPR-shaped at the surface and materially lighter underneath — except on consent quality, children, breach notification and prescribed security controls, where it is stricter.

B. Common-law reformers

United Kingdom — UK GDPR + Data Protection Act 2018, as amended by the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025

Royal Assent 19 June 2025; commencement is phased, with the Commencement No. 6 Regulations made on 29 January 2026 bringing much of the data protection package into force, and the complaint-handling duty biting from 19 June 2026.

What DUAA changes. A statutory list of "recognised legitimate interests" that need no balancing test (national security, crime prevention, safeguarding, emergencies); relaxed rules on purpose compatibility for further processing; a lighter touch on solely automated decision-making — Art. 22's general prohibition is replaced by safeguards, except for special-category data; a "stop the clock" mechanism for subject access requests pending identity verification or clarification; a new duty on controllers to operate a complaints process and respond within 30 days; smart data schemes and digital verification services; and the abolition of the ICO, replaced by a board-governed Information Commission with enhanced powers, including PECR fines raised to the GDPR level of £17.5 million or 4%.

India comparison. The UK is the clearest example of divergence-by-simplification from the GDPR while retaining its architecture. India performed a much more radical version of the same move — but where the UK loosened controller obligations while keeping individual remedies intact (including compensation and an independent regulator), India loosened obligations and removed remedies.

Australia — Privacy Act 1988, as amended by the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024

Scope. APP entities: agencies and organisations with turnover above A$3 million, plus specified others — the notorious small business exemption, unique among developed economies.

2024–26 position. Tranche 1 passed in December 2024: a statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy (intrusion upon seclusion and misuse of information), which commenced 10 June 2025; a Children's Online Privacy Code to be developed by the OAIC; automated decision-making transparency obligations in privacy policies (with a longer lead time); a criminal offence of doxxing; and tiered civil penalties including new mid-tier and infringement-notice powers. Tranche 2 — removing the small business exemption, introducing a right to erasure, and adding a fair-and-reasonable-processing test — remains a Government commitment with no bill passed and no commencement date as of mid-2026.

India comparison. Australia is India's mirror image on redress: Australia added a private cause of action with no comprehensive consent overhaul; India built a comprehensive consent regime with no private cause of action at all. Australia also shows what an exemption for small business costs politically once it becomes entrenched — relevant to India's S.17(3) startup exemption power.

Canada — PIPEDA 1985/2000, Quebec Law 25

Federal. PIPEDA remains in force: a principles-based, consent-centred statute built on the CSA Model Code, with an Ombudsman-model Privacy Commissioner who cannot issue fines. Bill C-27 (the Consumer Privacy Protection Act + AI and Data Act) died on the Order Paper when Parliament was prorogued in January 2025, and comprehensive federal reform has not been re-enacted since. Canada therefore has the weakest enforcement posture of any G7 country.

Quebec. Law 25 (in force in stages 2022–2024) is the real Canadian reform: consent modernisation, privacy by default, breach reporting, data portability, automated-decision transparency, mandatory privacy impact assessments, and penalties up to C$25 million or 4% of worldwide turnover.

India comparison. Canada is a cautionary case in how easily comprehensive reform dies. India's own 2019 Bill died the same way; the difference is that India came back with a shorter statute that could actually pass.

United States — no comprehensive federal law

Federal. Sectoral only: HIPAA (health), GLBA (financial), FCRA (credit), COPPA (children under 13), VPPA, TCPA, plus FTC Act §5 unfairness/deception enforcement, which functions as the de facto general privacy regulator. Multiple comprehensive federal bills (ADPPA, APRA) have failed.

States. As of mid-2026, 20 comprehensive state privacy laws are in effect, with Indiana, Kentucky and Rhode Island commencing on 1 January 2026; Alabama enacted the 21st comprehensive framework and Vermont's Data Privacy and Online Surveillance Act was signed on 16 June 2026. The common template — opt-out of sale/sharing/targeted advertising, opt-in for sensitive data, access/delete/correct/portability, universal opt-out signal recognition, data protection assessments — traces to Virginia's CDPA, with California's CCPA/CPRA as the stricter outlier (a dedicated regulator in the CPPA, employee and B2B data in scope, a limited private right of action for breaches, and ADMT and risk-assessment regulations). Layered on top: state health-data laws (Washington's My Health My Data, with a full private right of action), biometric laws (Illinois BIPA, still the largest source of privacy class action exposure in the world), and age-appropriate-design and social-media-minor statutes, many of them in First Amendment litigation.

India comparison. The US is the counterfactual India avoided: fragmentation. India's single national statute with express override of inconsistent laws (S.38) buys coherence at the cost of the US model's two genuine strengths — an enforcement culture with real penalties, and private litigation as a discovery engine for bad practice.

C. Asia-Pacific

China — PIPL (2021), with the Cybersecurity Law, Data Security Law and 2025–26 instruments

Scope. Extraterritorial where processing outside China aims to provide products or services to natural persons in China, analyses or assesses their behaviour, or in other prescribed circumstances (Art. 3).

Lawful bases (Art. 13). Consent; contractual necessity or HR management; legal obligation; public health emergencies or protection of life/property; news reporting and public-interest media within reasonable limits; publicly disclosed data within reasonable limits; and other statutory grounds. Note: no legitimate interests — China and India agree here, and both therefore lean heavily on consent.

Distinctive features. Separate consent requirements for sensitive personal information, cross-border transfers, public disclosure and third-party sharing — a consent-granularity model India has not adopted. Sensitive personal information is defined by harm (biometrics, religion, specific identity, medical health, financial accounts, location, and all personal information of minors under 14), not by an enumerated list alone. Automated decision-making is regulated (Art. 24: transparency, fairness, a right to refuse purely automated marketing, and a right to explanation). Large platform operators carry extra duties including independent oversight bodies and periodic social-responsibility reports.

Transfers (Art. 38). Three routes: a CAC security assessment (mandatory above data volume thresholds and for CIIOs), a CAC standard contract with filing, or certification. The Measures for Certification of Cross-Border Personal Information Transfer, issued by CAC and SAMR on 17 October 2025, took effect 1 January 2026, opening the certification route to mid-scale exporters; supplementary national standards become mandatory from 1 July 2026. Amendments to the Cybersecurity Law effective in 2026 increased penalties for non-compliant transfers.

Enforcement. CAC-led, with sectoral regulators. Fines up to ¥50 million or 5% of prior- year turnover, plus business suspension, licence revocation, and personal liability for responsible individuals up to ¥1 million and director disqualification.

India comparison. China is the closest structural analogue to India on state posture — broad national-security carve-outs and executive control — but the opposite on data flows (strict outbound control vs. India's negative list) and on sensitive data (a harm-based definition vs. India having none). China also has what India lacks: a functioning, aggressive regulator and a rule on automated decisions.

Japan — APPI (2003, amended 2015, 2020, and 2026)

Current law. A business-friendly regime: consent is the default for third-party provision but an opt-out filing route exists; "anonymously processed information" and "pseudonymously processed information" are formal statutory categories with relaxed rules; breach notification to the Personal Information Protection Commission (PPC) and affected individuals is mandatory for specified categories of leak. Japan holds a mutual adequacy arrangement with the EU.

The 2026 amendment. Cabinet-approved and submitted to the Diet on 7 April 2026, passed both houses on 10 July 2026, promulgated 17 July 2026, to come into force by Cabinet order within two years (so by July 2028 at the latest). It:

  • creates a consent exemption for statistical processing, expressly to enable AI development, including collecting publicly available sensitive data and sharing personal data with third parties for such purposes, subject to transparency and contractual safeguards;
  • adds children's protections: parental consent for under-16s and enhanced deletion and suspension rights;
  • creates "Specific Biometric Personal Information" (e.g. facial recognition data) with heightened transparency, expanded deletion rights, and a prohibition on opt-out third-party provision;
  • expands PPC enforcement, including flexible orders and administrative fines for serious violations — a significant departure for a regime that has historically relied on guidance and orders.

India comparison. Japan is doing explicitly what India left implicit: legislating an AI-training carve-out and a biometric category. India has neither, and its Rule 13 algorithmic due-diligence duty is a far cruder instrument than Japan's targeted exemption-plus-safeguards design.

South Korea — PIPA (2011, major amendment 2023, further amendments 2026)

Among the strictest regimes in Asia. Opt-in consent as the norm with granular separate-consent requirements; unique identifier restrictions (resident registration numbers); mandatory breach notification within 72 hours; a right to data portability (the "MyData" transmission right, extended economy-wide in the 2023 amendment); rights concerning automated decisions, including a right to refuse or to request an explanation of decisions that significantly affect rights; and a powerful, genuinely independent Personal Information Protection Commission (PIPC) that has fined Google, Meta and domestic platforms heavily.

2026 amendments. On 12 February 2026 the National Assembly passed amendments authorising administrative fines of up to 10% of a company's total revenue in certain high-severity breach cases; the amendment was promulgated on 10 March 2026, with most provisions taking effect 11 September 2026, and includes clarified CEO accountability, designation of a Chief Privacy Officer, and mandatory ISMS-P certification for private entities from 1 July 2027.

India comparison. Korea shows the ceiling India stopped short of: turnover-linked penalties, an independent commission, portability, and automated-decision rights. It is also the strongest evidence against the argument that strict privacy law suppresses a digital economy.

Singapore — PDPA 2012, amended 2020

A deliberately pragmatic middle path. Consent-based but with a statutory legitimate interests exception and a business improvement exception added in 2020; deemed consent by notification for certain processing; mandatory breach notification to the PDPC and affected individuals for breaches of significant scale (≥ 500 individuals) or likely significant harm; a data portability obligation legislated but not yet in force; mandatory DPO appointment for every organisation; and financial penalties up to 10% of annual turnover in Singapore or S$1 million, whichever is higher. The PDPC pairs enforcement with extensive practical guidance and a Trustmark certification.

India comparison. Singapore is the model India's business lobby wanted and partly got: light, certain, and pro-innovation. But Singapore kept a legitimate interests exception and a universal DPO requirement, both of which India omitted — an odd pair of omissions, since the first is what makes a light regime workable and the second is what makes it auditable.

Indonesia — Personal Data Protection Law (Law 27/2022)

GDPR-shaped: controller/processor split, six lawful bases including legitimate interests, specific personal data (health, biometrics, genetics, criminal records, children's data, financial data, sexual orientation), DPO requirements, 3×24-hour breach notification, transfer rules based on adequacy/appropriate safeguards/consent, criminal sanctions for unlawful collection and disclosure, and administrative fines up to 2% of annual revenue. The two-year transition ended in October 2024, but the supervisory authority contemplated by the law had a protracted establishment process, leaving an enforcement gap of the same kind India now has.

Vietnam — Law on Personal Data Protection (Law 91/2025/QH15)

Passed 26 June 2025, in force 1 January 2026, with an implementing Decree issued 31 December 2025. It replaces Decree 13/2023 with a full statute: consent-centred with narrow exceptions, express prohibition on buying and selling personal data, mandatory impact assessment dossiers filed with the Ministry of Public Security for both processing and cross-border transfers, sensitive-data categories, and sectoral rules (credit, health, biometrics, location, advertising). Penalties are unusually structured: up to 10 times the illicit gain, 5% of prior-year revenue for cross-border transfer violations, and up to VND 3 billion otherwise. Micro-enterprises, small businesses and start-ups get a five-year exemption from certain obligations.

India comparison. Vietnam legislated in the same window as India's rules and went the other way on almost everything: filing-based transfer control instead of a negative list, an explicit data-sale ban, revenue-linked fines. The one convergence is the small-business grace period — India's S.17(3)/(4) powers are the same idea, exercisable by notification.

Thailand — PDPA 2019 (fully in force 1 June 2022)

A close GDPR transposition: six-plus lawful bases including legitimate interests, sensitive data categories that notably include race, religion, political opinion, criminal records, disability, trade union, genetic, biometric and health, DPO requirements, 72-hour breach notification, transfer restrictions with adequacy/BCR/SCC-equivalents, and administrative fines up to THB 5 million plus criminal liability for unlawful sensitive-data disclosure and punitive damages up to twice actual damages in civil claims.

D. Latin America, Africa and the Middle East

Brazil — LGPD (Law 13,709/2018, in force 2020; sanctions from August 2021)

The most faithful GDPR transposition outside Europe. Ten lawful bases (including legitimate interests and credit protection), sensitive data categories, full rights set including portability and a right to review of automated decisions, DPO ("encarregado") requirement, and an independent-ish authority, the ANPD, converted into an autonomous agency in 2022. Sanctions: warnings, fines up to 2% of Brazilian group revenue capped at BRL 50 million per infraction, daily fines, publicisation, blocking and deletion of data, and partial or total suspension of database operations. ANPD's 2024 legitimate-interests guidance sets a three-stage balancing test, and it has been active on international transfer rules and on AI-training-related enforcement.

India comparison. Brazil is the proof that a developing economy can adopt the full GDPR model. India explicitly chose not to. The clearest divergence: Brazil kept individual redress and an autonomous regulator; India kept neither.

Saudi Arabia — Personal Data Protection Law (2021, amended 2023)

Enforced by SDAIA with a grace period that ended 14 September 2024. Consent-centred with narrow legitimate-interest exceptions (never for sensitive data), broad sensitive-data definitions, registration/records duties for controllers, mandatory DPO in defined cases, 72-hour breach notification to SDAIA, and transfer rules requiring adequacy or risk assessment. Penalties include criminal liability (up to 2 years' imprisonment) for disclosure of sensitive data with intent to harm, and fines up to SAR 5 million, doubled for repeat offences.

United Arab Emirates — Federal Decree-Law 45/2021, plus DIFC and ADGM regimes

The federal PDPL is GDPR-shaped but its Executive Regulations remained unissued for years, leaving the federal regime largely unenforceable in practice. The financial free zones do the real work: the DIFC Data Protection Law 2020 (with a 2022 amendment adding Article 10 duties on high-risk processing) and the ADGM Data Protection Regulations 2021 are independently supervised, GDPR-equivalent, and actively enforced.

India comparison. The UAE is the closest parallel to India's current situation — a notified statute waiting on the institutional layer. It also illustrates the risk: a law that sits unenforced for years trains the market to ignore it.

Nigeria — Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 + General Application and Implementation Directive (GAID) 2025

The NDPA created the Nigeria Data Protection Commission (NDPC) with real teeth. The GAID, issued 20 March 2025 and effective 19 September 2025, is the operational instrument: it sets thresholds for Data Controllers/Processors of Major Importance (based on data subject volume and sector), mandates annual compliance audit returns, requires DPO appointment, licenses Data Protection Compliance Organisations, and details breach notification (72 hours to the Commission) and transfer conditions. Penalties reach the higher of ₦10 million or 2% of annual gross revenue for major-importance entities.

Enforcement has been unusually vigorous for a young regime: in 2025 the NDPC issued compliance notices to 1,368 organisations across banking (795), insurance (35), insurance broking (392), gaming (136) and pensions (10), and it has tightened audit enforcement into 2026.

India comparison. Nigeria is the sharpest counterexample to the claim that developing- economy regulators cannot enforce. Nigeria's law is younger than India's and its regulator was running sector-wide sweeps while India's Board still had no members.

South Africa — POPIA (2013, fully in force 1 July 2021)

Eight statutory "conditions for lawful processing," an Information Regulator with enforcement-notice powers, special personal information categories including race and religious belief (constitutionally significant in the South African context), children's data protections, prior-authorisation requirements for certain high-risk processing, and criminal offences carrying up to 10 years' imprisonment for the most serious breaches, plus administrative fines up to ZAR 10 million.

Kenya — Data Protection Act 2019

GDPR-derived, with an Office of the Data Protection Commissioner that has been notably active in the East African region: mandatory registration of controllers and processors above thresholds, 72-hour breach notification, data protection impact assessments, transfer restrictions requiring proof of adequate safeguards or consent, and penalties up to KES 5 million or 1% of annual turnover, whichever is lower.

Switzerland — revised Federal Act on Data Protection (revFADP), in force 1 September 2023

Aligned to the GDPR sufficiently to preserve EU adequacy, but lighter: no administrative fines on companies — instead criminal fines of up to CHF 250,000 levied on responsible individuals, which produces a distinctive personal-accountability dynamic. Covers only natural persons since the revision (legal persons were dropped), requires records of processing, DPIAs, privacy by design and by default, and breach notification "as soon as possible."
