Reading. India is the outlier. Every other regime studied treats some data as categorically riskier. India's uniform-protection design is defensible in theory — it avoids the definitional arbitrage of category lists — but in the Indian context it means caste, religious identity, health status and biometric identifiers carry no additional statutory protection, in a country where those attributes carry unusually acute discrimination risk and where Aadhaar has made biometric processing routine. It is also a regression from the 2011 SPDI Rules the Act displaced.

Reading. India sets the highest age threshold in the world and pairs it with the most categorical advertising and tracking ban. The practical friction is real: an Indian platform must treat a 17-year-old the way a US platform treats a 12-year-old, and to know who is 17 it must engage in identity verification that itself creates privacy risk. The Fourth Schedule exemptions relieve schools, hospitals and childcare providers, but not consumer internet services. Expect S.9(5) — the power to notify a lower age for a class of Fiduciary — to be the pressure valve.

Reading. India's rights set is the thinnest of any comprehensive regime studied, and it is the only one that runs the accountability arrow in both directions. Two absences matter most in practice: portability (which is what makes competition policy and data-driven switching work — and which India has actually built infrastructure for, in Account Aggregator and ONDC, without granting the right) and automated-decision rights (in an economy where algorithmic credit scoring, insurance pricing and welfare eligibility determination are already at scale).

Reading. India's regime is the strictest in the table on two axes at once: no materiality filter and immediate individual notification. For a large consumer platform this converts routine, low-impact incidents — a misdirected email, a single-record access anomaly — into mandatory dual notifications carrying a ₹200 crore ceiling for failure. Expect this to be the first provision the industry seeks to soften, and expect over-notification to be the observed behaviour in the meantime.

Reading. India sits with the US at the permissive end — a deliberate signal to global services and to India's own IT/BPM export sector (reinforced by the S.17(1)(d) outsourcing exemption). The latent risk is that the negative list and the SDF localisation categories are both unbounded executive powers that can be exercised overnight, so the permissiveness is policy, not entitlement. A prudent architecture keeps transfer flows documentable and re-routable even though nothing currently requires a transfer mechanism.

Reading. Two-year renewable terms are the weakest tenure protection in the table. A member who wishes to be reappointed is structurally disincentivised from ruling against the Government that reappoints them — precisely when the Board's most important cases will involve State instrumentalities processing under S.7(b)–(e). Combined with S.17(2)(a) exemptions and Rule 23 information demands, this is the principal institutional-design criticism of the Indian regime, and the one most likely to be tested against Puttaswamy's procedural-safeguards limb.

Reading. India's headline number is large and its structure is weak. Absolute caps under-deter the largest firms and over-deter mid-market Indian ones. More importantly, India is the only jurisdiction in this table where an individual harmed by a data breach has no statutory monetary remedy of any kind — the penalty goes to the Consolidated Fund, S.43A of the IT Act was repealed, and S.39 bars civil courts from matters the Board can decide. Deterrence rests entirely on a regulator that does not yet exist.

Reading. India inverts the usual pattern. Most regimes impose light-but-universal accountability duties (records, DPO, DPIA for everyone above a threshold). India imposes almost nothing on the ordinary Fiduciary and a heavy, audited package on a small Government-designated set of SDFs — plus one universal, prescriptive, engineering-level security rule. The result is a compliance landscape with a very sharp cliff at the SDF boundary, and with Rule 6 as the only universal, testable technical standard. For any company prioritising work before May 2027, that makes Rule 6 the correct first investment.
