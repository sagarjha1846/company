Part one

The law in five minutes

India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act was passed in 2023 and sat dormant for two years. On 14 November 2025 the Rules were notified and the clock started.

Who's who

Three roles

A Data Principal is you. A Data Fiduciary is the company deciding what to do with your data — the word is deliberate: it implies a duty of trust. A Data Processor works for the Fiduciary.

A Significant Data Fiduciary is one the government names as big or risky enough for extra duties.

The core rule

Consent, or nothing much else

There are only two lawful grounds: your consent, or one of nine narrow "legitimate uses" — mostly for the State and for employment.

There is no "legitimate interests" ground like Europe's. So analytics, personalisation and product improvement all need consent, separately and specifically.

The money

Up to ₹250 crore

Per instance, for failing to keep data secure. ₹200 crore for failing to report a breach, or for mishandling a child's data.

None of it goes to you. Penalties go to the Consolidated Fund, and the Act gives individuals no compensation right at all.

What actually changes for a company

Notices get rebuilt from scratch

Every consent needs a standalone, itemised notice — not a clause buried in terms of service — listing exactly what data and exactly what purpose, available in English plus 22 Indian languages at the user's choice.

Security stops being a judgement call

India names the controls: encryption or tokenisation, access control, access logs kept at least a year, tested backups, and the same imposed on every vendor. Europe's "appropriate measures" language left room to argue. This does not.

Every breach gets reported

No severity threshold. Every affected person must be told without delay, the regulator too, with full particulars in 72 hours — and a separate six-hour report to CERT-In for cyber incidents.

Under-18s are off limits for advertising

Verifiable parental consent for everyone under 18 — the strictest age line in the world — plus a flat ban on tracking and targeted ads aimed at children.

The dates

14 Nov 2025
Rules notified, clock starts

The Board is created in law. The RTI amendment takes effect immediately — narrowing transparency before any privacy protection arrives.

Today · 19 Aug 2026
Nothing substantive is enforceable yet

The sections giving the Board its powers were never commenced, and it has no members. The old regime — the 2011 SPDI Rules — is still technically in force.

13 Nov 2026
Consent Manager registration opens

The new licensed intermediary becomes real. Except the registrar — the Board — may still not be staffed.

13 May 2027
Everything applies

Notice, consent, security, breach reporting, rights, children, retention. MeitY has proposed pulling large firms forward to November 2026.

Three regulators, one consent screen

This is the part almost every compliance programme misses. The same button is governed by three Indian regimes, enforced by three authorities — and the DPDP standard is the only one not yet enforceable.

Who is watching the same screen
RegimeAuthorityThe question it asksStatus
DPDP Act + RulesData Protection BoardIs consent free, specific, informed, unconditional? Is withdrawal as easy as giving?May 2027
Dark Patterns Guidelines, 2023Central Consumer Protection AuthorityDoes the interface manipulate the user into a choice they didn’t intend?Enforcing now
Competition Act, 2002Competition Commission of IndiaIs consent extracted by a dominant firm as a condition of access?Enforcing now

India was among the first countries anywhere to issue a dedicated dark-patterns instrument, in November 2023. It names thirteen patterns — false urgency, basket sneaking, confirm shaming, forced action, subscription trap, interface interference, and more. At least seven are consent-screen patterns. It is, in effect, a UI specification for lawful consent, written two years before the privacy standard it anticipates. Enforcement is live: a 2025 advisory forced three-month self-audits, and penalties have landed across aviation, e-commerce and digital healthcare.

All three converge on one rule: you cannot condition the service on consent to unrelated processing. Do it in India today and it is simultaneously an unfair trade practice, possibly an abuse of dominance, and — from 2027 — void consent.

The thing everyone gets wrong

"No regulator" does not mean no enforcement. The largest privacy penalty in Indian history — ₹213 crore against Meta, over WhatsApp's 2021 policy — came from the competition regulator, not a privacy one. It held that data sharing beyond running the service cannot be a condition of using it — which is almost exactly what the DPDP Act's consent standard will require. The tribunal upheld the penalty; the Supreme Court is supervising compliance now.

Part two

The strange institution at the centre of it

India legislated a company that is paid by businesses and legally accountable to their users. That is normally a conflict of interest. Here it is the licence condition.

It is called a Consent Manager. It registers with the Board, gets integrated by companies, and lets a person give, review and withdraw consent across all of them from one place. It routes consent without ever reading the data — a "data-blind" pipe, modelled on the Account Aggregator system that already moves financial data for millions of Indians.

The statute is explicit that it is accountable to the Data Principal — the individual — not to the company paying the bill. No other country has legislated this.

To register

The bar

Indian company · ₹2 crore net worth · interoperable platform · fit-and-proper promoters · published business model · registration granted by the Board.

Proven at scale

The precedent

17 licensed Account Aggregators already run this architecture in financial services. The hard part isn't the technology — it's whether ordinary people adopt it.

The catch

No registrar

Rule 4 switches on in November 2026. Registration is granted by a Board that has no chairperson and no members. A commenced rule with no registrar cannot be complied with.

How big is this, honestly

Third-party research puts the India data governance market at about $219M in 2025, ~$252M in 2026, reaching $765M by 2034 at roughly 14.9% CAGR. Broader framings run to billions — a $5.34B "data protection market" in 2024 — but those sweep in backup, disaster recovery and security tooling that has nothing to do with consent.

Use the narrow number. A realistic market for consent-and-rights infrastructure in India is in the low hundreds of millions of dollars a year — large enough to build a real company, and too small to support the dozen platforms currently in the category. Consolidation is already visible and will continue. The strategic conclusion: don’t plan to win a compliance-tooling market that will be commoditised. The durable asset is the consumer relationship — which appears in none of these sizings, because in India it does not yet exist.

The opening

Both markets are consolidating, and both are structurally single-sided. Privy and Securiti sell to the company. DeleteMe and Incogni sell to the person. Nobody is building one ledger with two faces — because in every other jurisdiction that would be a conflict of interest. India is the one place where the statute makes it a licence condition.

Part four · the product

One ledger, two faces

A single append-only record of every consent — purpose, notice version, timestamp, language, the action taken, every withdrawal, every downstream share. Data-blind: it holds the consent and the metadata, never the personal data. Everything else is a surface on that spine.

Side A · the company

For the Data Fiduciary

Meta. Or a bank, a hospital, a school, a D2C brand.

  1. Notice serviceStandalone, itemised notices generated in English plus 22 languages from a structured purpose registry, versioned and hashed. The most under-estimated build in the Act.
  2. Consent SDKsWeb, Android, iOS, server — with withdrawal parity enforced in the component. And the thirteen dark patterns made impossible to ship: enforced symmetry, no pre-ticking, purpose separation, single-prompt. That turns three regulators into a build-time constraint instead of a review-time argument.
  3. Purpose & basis registryEvery data element mapped to consent or a named legitimate use. Doubles as the GDPR Article 30 record.
  4. Rights fulfilmentIncluding the recipient ledger — who the data was shared with and what — which competitors under-build because it needs real sharing telemetry. Plus nomination, which nobody supports.
  5. Retention engineField-level statutory retention map, purpose-expiry deletion, and the three-year inactivity clock with its 48-hour pre-erasure notice.
  6. Breach consoleTwo decision trees, a pre-built affected-user query, and the three clocks: six hours, without delay, 72 hours.
  7. SDF packAnnual DPIA, audit evidence room, algorithmic due-diligence register, and a localisation switch that can pin a data category to India on short notice.

Side B · the person

For the Data Principal

The person who uses Meta. On a mid-range Android, in their own language, free.

  1. See every consent you've givenAcross every integrated company — what data, what purpose, since when. Most people have never seen this list.
  2. Withdraw in one tapPropagating to the company and its processors, with a receipt you keep.
  3. Exercise rights without writing an emailAccess, correction, erasure — and the internal grievance step the law makes a mandatory precondition before you can complain to the regulator.
  4. Nominate someoneTo hold your rights if you die or are incapacitated. A genuinely novel Indian right that nobody has shipped.
  5. Breach inboxEvery notification you're owed, in plain language, in your language, with what you can actually do about it.
  6. Parent controlsOne place to grant and revoke verifiable parental consent, and see every service holding your child's data.
  7. EscalateWhen a company misses its own published deadline, a one-tap complaint to the Board with the precondition already evidenced.

The spec nobody has written

Rule 4 requires an interoperable platform. Nobody has published what that means — no artefact schema, no registry, no answer to how a company discovers which Consent Manager a given person uses. India does have three lineages to build on: MeitY’s Electronic Consent Framework, which established consent as a signed machine-readable artefact rather than a checkbox event; DEPA’s data-blind intermediary pattern; and the only one running in production — ReBIT’s Account Aggregator API specification, with separate catalogues for the aggregator, the data holder and the data requester, roles that map cleanly onto DPDP.

Publishing a workable interface first is how a private spec becomes the default. The Account Aggregator ecosystem’s own history shows how much power sits with whoever writes the reference implementation.

The requirement that separates real products from compliance logs

MeitY’s own Business Requirement Document (June 2025) asks for real-time consent verification over secure APIs, with the data request blocked immediately where consent is missing, expired or withdrawn. That makes consent an authorisation service in the request path, not an audit log consulted afterwards. It is technically hard, most CMP vendors are not built for it, and it is where a genuine moat sits.

The second hard thing is the recipient ledger — who your data was actually shared with, and what. It needs sharing telemetry rather than a consent record, no existing specification covers it, and it is what makes the access right real. It is also the single most compelling thing a consumer product can show someone: where their data went.

A distinction vendors are already blurring

Being shortlisted or empanelled by NeGD is not the same as being registered as a Consent Manager by the Data Protection Board under Rule 4. The first is a government innovation and procurement track. The second is the statutory licence — and it does not open until 13 November 2026, before a Board exists to grant it. Read any “government-approved consent manager” claim carefully.

Why two sides beat one

The user side is the moat

Consent intermediation has network effects: the useful Consent Manager is the one users already have and companies already integrate. Consumer adoption is the hard half — and the defensible half.

The company side pays for it

Enterprise contracts fund a free consumer product. The consumer product is not charity; it is the distribution and the defensibility.

Each side sells the other

Every consent screen rendered for a company is a distribution surface: manage this later in your consent wallet. Every user is a reason for the next company to integrate.

The statute clears the conflict

Rule 4 makes accountability to the individual a registration condition. What would look like double-dealing anywhere else is the licence requirement here.

First 90 days

  1. The purpose registry and consent ledger. Append-only, exportable, hash-versioned.
  2. The notice renderer — 22-language output from a structured purpose definition. Sellable on its own, and nobody does it well.
  3. Web and Android consent SDKs with withdrawal parity enforced in the component.
  4. The breach console, seeded with both decision trees and the three-clock timer.
  5. A read-only consumer view — one link from any consent screen showing a person what they agreed to. Not the full app: just enough to prove the two-sided thesis and start the network effect.
